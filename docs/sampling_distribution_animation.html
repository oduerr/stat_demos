Sampling Distribution of \(\hat{\mu}\)

Manim animation: how the distribution of the estimator \(\hat{\mu} = \bar{X}\) emerges from repeated sampling
← Back to STAT Demos

This animation illustrates the concept of a sampling distribution in the context of parametric estimation. We assume the data are drawn from a Gaussian (normal) distribution with two unknown parameters: \[ X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu,\, \sigma^2), \quad i = 1, \ldots, n. \] Our goal is to estimate the parameter \(\mu\) from the data.

We use the sample mean as our estimator: \[ \hat{\mu} = \bar{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i. \] Here \(\hat{\mu}\) (read: "mu hat") denotes that this is an estimate of the true but unknown parameter \(\mu\). It is in fact the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) of \(\mu\) for a Gaussian model.

Because \(\hat{\mu}\) is computed from random data, it is itself a random variable. The animation builds up the sampling distribution of \(\hat{\mu}\) by repeating the following experiment many times: draw a fresh sample of size \(n\), compute \(\hat{\mu}\), and record the result. The histogram of these values approximates the sampling distribution of \(\hat{\mu}\).

The estimator \(\hat{\mu} = \bar{X}\) is unbiased — its expected value equals the true parameter regardless of \(\sigma\): \[ \mathbb{E}[\hat{\mu}] = \mu. \]

Because \(\hat{\mu}\) is a continuous random variable, its sampling distribution is described by a probability density function (PDF). A valid PDF must satisfy: \[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{\hat{\mu}}(x)\,dx = 1. \] In the animation, the area under the histogram should therefore integrate to 1.

There is a small error in the sampling distribution shown in the animation — can you spot it?

The histogram bars are displayed with their raw counts (or relative frequencies) on the \(y\)-axis rather than a proper probability density. As a result, the area under the displayed histogram does not integrate to 1 — it integrates to the bin width times the total number of samples (or to 1 only if relative frequencies happen to equal the density values).

To make the histogram a proper probability density, each bar height must be divided by \((\text{total samples}) \times (\text{bin width})\), so that the sum of all bar areas equals 1: \[ \sum_k \frac{\text{count}_k}{N \cdot \Delta x} \cdot \Delta x = \frac{1}{N}\sum_k \text{count}_k = 1. \] In other words, the \(y\)-axis should show density, not frequency.
